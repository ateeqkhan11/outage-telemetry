How an independent outage trigger would have handled 16 AWS us-east-1 outages

Backtest pack · draft for review · built 1 November 2026 · AWS us-east-1 (N. Virginia), 2011–2026

We replayed every us-east-1 outage AWS has published a post-event summary for, plus four widely reported ones without a summary, against our draft trigger. The trigger needs probes run by two independent operators to see the same AWS service failing. The question for each outage: would it have paid after a 1-hour or a 2-hour waiting period?

What we found

  • Clear, total outages pay under any rule. The 2017 S3 outage and the October 2025 DynamoDB failure would have paid after both waiting periods in every simulation.
  • Most outages are partial, and a simple per-minute rule misses them. Some calls keep working. Checked once a minute, a partial outage looks like flickering and rarely adds up to an hour. That affects the 2015, 2020, 2021 and 2023 events.
  • Six checks a minute, judged over a rolling 10 minutes, catches them. With that rule, every outage our checks can see that lasted over 2 hours pays, in every simulation. This is our recommendation for trigger v1.
  • 7 of 16 outages wouldn't be seen. 4 were confined to one availability zone, and 3 hit services outside our five checks (ELB, SimpleDB, Kinesis/CloudWatch). This is the basis risk to discuss with buyers. Adding checks for those services and zones is cheap, and it's the Q1 plan.
  • Zone outages can still show up. In 2011 and October 2012 one zone's failure also degraded the region-wide EC2 API, which our check measures, so those pay too.
  • Frequency. With the recommended rule and a 2-hour wait, about 6 regional events in 16 years would have paid, 4 of them since 2020.

The threshold is a pricing dial

The recommended rule counts an operator as seeing a failure when a set share of its checks over 10 minutes fail. A lower threshold pays on milder partial outages and costs more to cover. With a 2-hour wait: 8 events pay at 10%, 3 at 25% under both assumptions, and 2 at 50%.

Must-not-pay tests

An independent trigger has to ignore problems that aren't AWS's. Each case below ran for 3 hours through the same detection code.

Numbers are the longest outage each rule reported. GitHub Actions runs on Microsoft Azure, so GitHub and Azure probes count as one operator. A failed control check (a site outside AWS) marks the probe as down rather than AWS. An HTTP 403 means AWS answered, so it never counts as an outage.

How this was done, and its limits

  • Sources. AWS's own post-event summaries for 12 events. Press and status-page reports for the 4 marked "press only".
  • Simulated probes. Our probes didn't exist during these events, and AWS publishes service-level descriptions, not per-call error rates. So we simulated three independent probe operators minute by minute, and ran their results through the same detection code the live probes use.
  • Assumed failure rates per call:
    • "Largely unavailable": 95%.
    • "Very high errors": 55%.
    • "Elevated errors" (partial): tested at both 20% and 50%.
    • Latency-only: 5% or 20%.
  • What we assumed about the probes. One network misses 15% of minutes, to reflect GitHub's late schedules. Background failures run at 0.05% of calls.
  • Judgement calls are written down in the code. For example, the 2017 Lambda impact is assumed to share S3's window. The 2021 STS impairment is treated as latency only. The 2024 Lambda impact doesn't count, because only functions that wait on logging were hurt.
  • Durations from the rolling rule can run a few minutes past the real end of an outage, because it looks back 10 minutes.
  • Reproduce it: python -m backtest.backtest in the outage-telemetry repository.
